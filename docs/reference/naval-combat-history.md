# Caribbean Naval Combat 1650-1720: Research for Corsair's Combat Model

Scope: buccaneer era (c. 1655-1690) through the "golden age" (c. 1714-1726). This covers ships, guns, refits, boarding and named captains, then ends with what it means for Corsair.

**How reliable the numbers are.** Figures marked [S#] come from sources fetched or searched for this report (list at the end). Figures marked [gen] are standard figures from the general naval-history literature (Lavery, Konstam, Cordingly, Little) that I did not re-check against a page this session. Treat them as good ballpark values, not citations. Period numbers vary a lot. Use ranges, not single values.

**One recurring warning.** Period accounts and later popular histories exaggerate pirate gun counts. Benerson Little points out that Blackbeard's "40-gun" *Queen Anne's Revenge*, at 200-300 tons, "was probably no more than a 24 to 26 gun ship of 6 pound and smaller shot, with the rest being swivel guns", and the guns recovered from the wreck are small [S1]. Ships also commonly carried fewer guns than they had ports [S1].

### 1.2 Important types Corsair lacks

- **Periagua / pirogue / canoe.** The early buccaneer's real attack craft. Dugouts up to ~40 ft x 8 ft, rowed or sailed, "very close to the wind… within three points" [S1]. They carry no cannon, so the crew relies on musketry and boarding [S16]. This is how Morgan- and Sawkins-era raiders took Spanish ships.
- **Pinnace.** A small, fast, ship-rigged or two-masted vessel of 20-100 t that served as a tender or scout [gen]. A natural early-game alternative to the sloop.
- **Barque longue / barcalonga.** The Spanish and Mediterranean version was lug-rigged, which made it "an excellent vessel for chasing or escaping to windward" [S1]. The guarda costa of the period used these.
- **Snow.** The correct period word for a two-masted square-rigger after c. 1690 [S1].
- **Galley-frigate / "galley".** A ship with oar ports on the gundeck (*Adventure Galley*, *Whydah Gally*). French ships of up to 54 guns carried up to 30 sweeps [S1]. Oars give movement in a calm, which is decisive in Caribbean lulls.
- **Fireship.** Any old hull filled with combustibles and fitted with grapnels on the yardarms [S1][S13]. Morgan's decisive weapon.
- **Schooner.** Effectively absent before ~1716 and rare as a pirate ship [S1]. Keep it out of the 1660 start, or make it a late unlock.

### 1.3 Sailing and handling facts useful for the model

- **Windward ability by rig.** Square rig at best fills 6 points (67.5 deg) off the wind, and "most could keep their sails filled… between seven and eight points". Fore-and-aft rig manages ~4 points (45 deg) [S17].
- **Leeway** is typically 6-12 deg, about 1 point. So a square-rigger close-hauled at 7 points makes good ~8 points (90 deg) and gains **no ground to windward at all** [S17]. In practice, being to leeward of a sloop means you can never catch it.
- **Tacking a square-rigger was unreliable.** It needed speed beforehand and a well-drilled crew. Under-manned ships tacked badly [S17]. This is a direct case for linking crew numbers to manoeuvre.
- **In battle ships carried minimal sail** (topsails, perhaps the foresail, a headsail), which limited them to ~7 points [S1].
- **The weather gauge** chooses when to engage and blows smoke and burning wads onto the enemy. The lee gauge is better in high wind and seas (the lee guns can open their ports) and makes withdrawing easier [S1].

## 2. Gunnery

### 2.1 Gun types (17th-century names, and the pound ratings coming into use by 1700)

| Old name | Shot (lb) | Gun weight | Period use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Falcon / falconet | 1-2.5 | ~500 lb | Upper works, boats [S4] |
| Minion | ~4 | ~1,000 lb | Small merchantmen, sloops [gen] |
| Saker | 5-6 | 1,400-1,900 lb [S4][S18] | The workhorse of merchantmen, sloops and small frigates. One 1679 English ship carried sakers of 8, 10 and 16 cwt grouped by deck [S1] |
| Demi-culverin | 9-12 | up to 3,400 lb [S18] | Upper decks of warships, main guns of frigates |
| Culverin | 17.5-18 | 4,500 lb [S4] | Lower decks of 4th rates and large galleons. The biggest guns usually seen on Spanish ships [S1] |
| Demi-cannon | 32-33 | 4,000+ lb [S4] | Lower decks of 1st-3rd rates only. "very rarely" on 4th rates [S1] |
| Swivel / paterero / murderer | ~0.5-1 | small, breech-loading chambers | Rails, tops, bulkheads. Max ~320 yds; **effective ~133 yds** with roundshot [S1] |
| Coehorn mortar | grenade | small | Pirate *Mocha Frigate* (1697) and Woodes Rogers carried them [S1] |

- **Carronades are 1770s+. Do not include them.** The gunlock (flint firing) was adopted by the RN only in 1745 [S19]. The period used linstocks and slow match [S1].
- **Iron vs bronze ("brass").** Bronze cost **3-4x** as much, and was lighter for the same shot (stronger metal, so thinner walls) [S20]. It was also safer: a bronze gun splits rather than shatters, while an iron gun throws shrapnel when it bursts [S20]. But bronze **overheats and droops if fired too fast** [S20]. Bronze does **not** shoot meaningfully farther or reload faster. Range comes from bore, length and charge, not alloy. Navies and Spanish treasure ships kept bronze for prestige ships. Merchants and pirates mostly had iron.
- **Powder.** Corned powder was standard. Period gunners distinguished cannon, musket and fine powder; fine powder was for pistols and priming [S1]. Damp, caked powder from long tropical cruises was a real problem: Little's tests of heat and humidity degraded it [S1]. Fresh, dry powder is a believable small range or reliability bonus. "Fine-grain = +15% range" is over-stated.

### 2.2 Ranges (the numbers period seamen actually used)

| Term | Distance | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Max range ("random shot", elevated) | 1,500-2,500 paces; ~2,000 yd in modern summaries [S4] | A saker reaches ~2,400 yd with a 4 lb charge [S18]. Useless for hitting a ship |
| Point blank (≈ "gunshot") | 150-200 paces for the common guns [S4]; demi-culverin 200 paces [S18] | The range where the shot goes where the gun points [S1]. Roughly 250-350 yd |
| Effective fighting range of a demi-culverin | ~550 m / 600 yd [S18] | Upper limit for hull hits |
| Musket shot | **700-800 ft (English), 700-1,000 ft (French)**; buccaneer gun (*fusil boucanier*) 800-1,000 ft [S1] | The normal distance at which ships "engaged" |
| Half-musket / pistol shot | ~35-70 yd; "yardarm to yardarm" ≈ 10 yd [S1] | Close action and boarding range |
| Case / grape shot | **Not beyond 60 yd** (Povey 1702). Pattern ~6 ft at 60 yd [S1] | Anti-personnel only at close range |

"Most engagements were fought at under 1,000 yards and sometimes within pistol shot (25 to 50 yards)" [S4]. **Real fights happened mostly inside 300 yd**, with decisive damage at 100 yd or less.

### 2.3 Rate of fire

- A trained late-18th-century RN broadside was 2-3 per ~5 minutes (about 1 per 2-3 min), slowing as crew fell [S19][S21]. Little live practice happened; captains drilled by running guns in and out [S21].
- In 1650-1720 assume **slower**: well-drilled navy crews ~1 broadside per 3-5 min [gen]. Spanish crews were notoriously slow. Guns were manned partly by soldiers, and gun crews were small (a 30-gun galleon had 66 men at the guns [S8], about 2 per gun, so one side only).
- **Merchant crews.** Under 20 men can't man more than a few guns. Expect 1 shot per gun per 5-10 min, or a single token broadside, then surrender [S10][gen].
- **Pirate crews** had 4x the hands of a merchantman [S10]. They could man both broadsides and the swivels and still put up a wall of musketry. Their gunnery was rarely drilled, though de Graaf's was an exception.
- Firing chase guns cost the chaser some way, and a chased ship lost way yawing to bring stern guns to bear. Duguay-Trouin saw a chase shift six guns to the stern [S1].

### 2.4 Ammunition and its purpose

| Ammo | Purpose | Period notes |
|---|---|---|
| Round shot | Hull, guns, men (by splinters) | Splinters caused more wounds than the shot. Big ships **reduced charges** so slow shot splintered rather than punched through [S1] |
| Bar, cross-bar and chain shot | Rigging and sails; slows or cripples the enemy [S4][S19] | The pirate and privateer choice: cripple the prize without holing it |
| Case, burrel, bags of iron, "small iron shot", later "grape" | Men on deck and boarders [S1][S19] | The *word* "grapeshot" only became common early in the 18th century, but small shot in bags was used throughout [S1]. Swept decks before boarding. Bulkhead "murderers" swept boarders |
| Langridge | Scrap and junk against crews [S19] | Pirates' improvised load. The *Mocha* fired tin and pewter [S1] |
| Grenades and firepots | Clearing decks before boarding. Fire | Clay or iron grenades kept in tubs. *La Belle* firepots each held an iron grenade [S1] |
| Heated shot | Setting ships on fire | Coastal forts, not ships at sea [S19] |

### 2.5 Aiming high vs low, and raking

- **French: high.** They fired at masts and rigging on the upward roll, often from the leeward position, to disable and escape or to choose the fight [S22].
- **English: low.** They fired into the hull on the down roll from the weather gauge to cause splinter casualties among the gun crews [S22]. (This is classically described for the 18th century, but the habit was already forming.)
- **Spanish.** They defended treasure and preferred close action and musketry by embarked soldiers; their gunnery was slow [gen].
- **Pirates and privateers.** The aim was an *undamaged* prize: dismast, shoot rigging, sweep the deck, board [S15][S16]. Leeson stresses that a damaged prize and dead pirates were costs [S15].
- **Raking** means firing along the length of the ship through the bow or the stern. The stern (glass galleries, light framing) was weakest. Ships rarely mounted more than a few stern chasers, so a raking position meant a whole broadside against 2-6 guns [S1][gen]. **Athwart the hawse** (across the bow) was favoured for boarding because "they could bring their guns to bear but the enemy could not" [S1].

## 4. Boarding, crew, morale and surrender

- **Most prizes surrendered without a fight.** Pirates promised **no quarter for resistance and mercy for surrender**, and carried it out to build a reputation. The Jolly Roger was a signal to that end [S15]. Crews refused to fight: "Wyers Men all declared they would not Fight… believing they would be Murthered" [S15]. Britain even legislated in 1721 to force merchantmen to resist [S15].
- **The numbers drove it.** The average pirate crew was ~80 men against usually under 20 on a merchantman, or 4x a 250-ton ship's need [S10]. Rediker: ~4,000 pirates on ~80 ships took ~2,400 vessels in 1716-26 [S10].
- **Pirates avoided damage.** Their own dead and wounded and a damaged prize were costs [S15].
- **Boarding sequence** (Little, Duguay-Trouin, Villehuet) [S1]:
  1. Close, usually from to windward or athwart the hawse. Lash the spritsail yard along the bowsprit so it can ram the shrouds.
  2. Musketry suppresses the enemy. Boarders **lie low and hold fire** until the grapnels go, to dodge the defenders' broadside.
  3. Grenades and firepots clear the deck. Grapnels go into the shrouds and the ships are lashed at the chain-plates. Guns are hauled in and ports closed.
  4. The boarding party is ~half the officers, a third of the small-arms men, and two men per gun. **At most 3/4 of the crew board**; the rest keep up fire.
  5. Board from the forecastle into the enemy shrouds. Fight in lines, not a melee.
  6. Some captains waited to board until they saw "blood run out of their scuppers" [S1].
- **Defence.** Close quarters (loopholed bulkheads), powder chests, swivels sweeping the deck, sallies, cutting away grapnels, fending off with booms [S1]. A fluyt's tumblehome made it awkward to board [S1]. Boarding in heavy seas "probably never actually successful" [S1].
- **Wounds** came mainly from small shot and splinters [S1].
- **The buccaneer musket** (*fusil boucanier*, long barrel) was the primary weapon. Buccaneers were famed marksmen who picked off helmsmen and gunners, then boarded [S1][S16].
- **Crew quality.** Buccaneers were hunter-marksmen. Golden-age pirates were ex-merchant and ex-navy seamen, many experienced. Navy crews were drilled at the guns. Spanish crews had many soldiers (good boarding defence, slow guns) [S8].
- **Morale and surrender.**
  - Merchant crews struck when outnumbered or facing the black flag [S15].
  - Warships struck after heavy loss: *Princesa* struck after ~50 casualties vs 8-9 [S29].
  - Pirate morale collapsed when the captain fell. Roberts' death ended Cape Lopez; many of his crew were drunk [S11].
- **The captain's role.** Pirate captains were elected and absolute **in action only**; the quartermaster ran everything else [S1]. Personal example mattered: Roberts fought in crimson damask [S11]. Navy captains' aggression varied: Benbow's captains refused to engage [S14].

## 5. Famous captains: what made each dangerous

| Captain (active) | Signature | Fighting-style hooks for Corsair |

| **Charles Vane** (1717-21) | **Escape artist.** Broke out past the governor's ships at Nassau with a fireship [gen] | Refuses even fights, uses fireships, flees superior force (his crew voted him out for cowardice) [gen] |

## 6. Implications for Corsair's combat model

1. **Range bands, not one range.** Model long (>600 yd: chance hits only), gunshot (250-350 yd: real hull hits), musket (~250 yd: small arms start) and pistol/boarding (<70 yd: case shot, swivels, grenades). Most decisive action falls within 300 yd [S1][S4].
2. **Ammo choice as the core tactical lever.** Use round (hull), bar/chain (rigging, i.e. speed and turning), case/langridge (crew, ≤60 yd [S1]). The pirate playstyle is "chain, then case, then board" to keep the prize intact [S15].
3. **Crew is the key stat.** It sets how many guns are manned (rate of fire), tacking reliability [S17], boarding strength and how often the target surrenders. A 15-man merchantman with 16 guns fires like a 4-gun ship. Pirates win by manning everything [S10].
4. **Surrender as the default outcome.** Compute a surrender check from relative crew, guns, flag (black flag or reputation), the captain's cruelty reputation and losses taken. Resisting a cruel pirate should hurt (the Leeson equilibrium [S15]). Most merchant engagements should end in a strike, not a sinking.
5. **Rig and windward matter more than raw speed.** Square ships ~6.5-7 points and fore-and-aft ~4-5 points, minus leeway [S17]. A sloop to windward is uncatchable; a ship of the line to leeward is harmless. This fits the "best sailing" pillar.
6. **Fouling replaces "copper sheathing".** Speed decays with days since careening (sloops every 2-3 months [S26]). Careening restores it; wood or lead sheathing slows worm damage. Copper becomes a late-game (1760s) anachronism or is removed.
7. **Fix the upgrade list's history.**
   - Copper sheathing (1761) [S23]: anachronism.
   - Cotton sails (19th c.) [S28]: anachronism. Rename to new flax canvas.
   - "Iron scantlings": rename to doubled planking or oak riders.
   - Bronze cannon: real effect is lighter, safe and costly, not faster or longer [S20].
   - Fine-grain powder: rename to "dry, fresh powder" and give a smaller, reliability-flavoured bonus.
   - Add sweeps, swivels, flush deck, pierce-for-guns, boarding nettings, close quarters and a fireship option.
8. **Ships differ by role, not just size.**
   - Fluyt: rich and hard to board.
   - Galleon: many soldiers, slow fire, few real guns for its ports [S1].
   - Frigate: fast hunter.
   - Sloop: fast, shallow, fragile.
   - Ship of the line: gunnery fortress, rare, wartime only.
9. **Nation flavour.** French AI aims high (dismast and escape), English AI aims low (crew-killing hull fire), Spanish AI relies on slow guns plus heavy musketry and boarding defence [S22][S8], pirates use rigging shot and board.
10. **Captain archetypes:**
    - Morgan: fleet and fireship.
    - l'Olonnais / Low: terror (surrender bonus, no-quarter backlash).
    - de Graaf: elite gunnery and running fights.
    - Roberts: aggressive squadron, morale tied to the captain.
    - Blackbeard: intimidation with light guns.
    - Bellamy: chaser.
    - Vane: escape artist.
    - Bonnet: inept.
